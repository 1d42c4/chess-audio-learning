Finding Targets Around the Enemy King

When you look toward the enemy king, what are you actually trying to attack? The king itself is the final target, but reaching it usually requires working on something nearby. That something might be a pawn, an entry square, a defender, or an escape route. Learning to identify these smaller targets makes attacking plans much clearer. Instead of moving pieces toward the king and hoping, you can explain what must change and why. This lesson will help you hear the position as a set of relationships, without needing a diagram or a sequence of coordinates.

Start by thinking of the king's shelter as a system. Pawns block lines and control squares. Knights and bishops protect entry points. Rooks and queens can exchange attacking pieces or defend from a distance. The king may also have a path to a safer area. An attack succeeds when this system loses the ability to answer your threats. You rarely need to destroy every part of the shelter. Often one key defender moves away, one pawn is exchanged, and a previously closed route becomes usable. Your task is to find which change matters most.

The most obvious target is a pawn directly in front of the king. Attacking that pawn can tie down defenders or prepare an opening of the shelter. Yet an attacked pawn is not necessarily weak. If several pieces protect it and your own pieces cannot increase pressure, you may accomplish little. A useful target must be reachable, and pressure on it should create a problem. Ask whether the defender has to make an unpleasant choice. If defending the pawn costs nothing and improves the opponent's coordination, your target may be less useful than it first appears.

Pawns that have advanced can create contact points. Imagine that the enemy king sits behind three pawns, and the middle pawn has moved forward. Your own pawn may be able to approach and attack it diagonally. This creates the possibility of an exchange that opens a line. The advanced pawn can also leave squares behind it that another enemy pawn no longer controls. These are two different opportunities: attacking the pawn itself, and using the squares it stopped protecting. Keep them separate in your thinking because the best plan may use one while ignoring the other.

A square can be a target even when it is empty. Suppose a square beside the enemy king can be reached by your queen and protected by your bishop. If the king cannot capture your queen there because the bishop protects her, that square may become an entry point for a check or a mating threat. The square matters because of the cooperation between your pieces and the king's limited replies. Merely placing a queen next to a king is not enough. You must know what protects the queen and how the opponent can respond.

An entry square and a weak square are related but not identical. A weak square is difficult for the opponent to control, especially with pawns. An entry square is one your piece can use to penetrate. A square may be weak but unreachable. Another may be defended yet usable through a tactical exchange. This distinction prevents a common mistake: spending many moves aiming at an attractive square that your pieces can never occupy safely. Every time you identify a weakness, also identify the route. If there is no route, you may need another target.

Color can help organize the search. Every bishop stays on the color of square where it began. If the opponent has exchanged the bishop that guards the dark squares, and the surrounding pawns also leave dark squares vulnerable, your dark squared bishop may become especially useful. A group of weak squares of one color is called a weak color complex. You do not need to memorize the term to use the idea. Listen for the pattern: several nearby entry points can be attacked by the same bishop, while the opponent lacks the matching defender.

Be careful with that pattern. Losing a bishop does not automatically create a losing color weakness. A knight, queen, rook, or pawn may defend the important squares. The king may have plenty of room. Your bishop may be blocked by your own pawns. The practical question is whether your pieces can use the missing coverage. Strategic descriptions are invitations to investigate, not verdicts. If you say that the enemy dark squares are weak, complete the thought by naming which piece can reach them and what threat it will create after arriving.

A defending piece can be the most important target of all. Imagine an enemy knight close to the king. It protects a queen entry square, covers an escape square, and can capture your attacking bishop. Removing or chasing that knight may do more than directly attacking any pawn. You might exchange it with a bishop, drive it away with a pawn, or create a threat that forces it to move. But evaluate the whole exchange. If capturing the knight allows another defender to recapture into an even stronger position, the apparent removal may not help.

Sometimes a defender has too many jobs. A rook might protect a pawn beside its king and also prevent your rook from entering along an open file. If you can attack one duty with a forcing move, the rook may have to abandon the other. This is an overloaded defender. The audio friendly way to recognize it is to name both responsibilities. Then ask whether the defender can continue doing both after your move. Do not assume that two jobs always mean overload. Many pieces comfortably defend several things when those duties do not conflict.

Pinned defenders deserve a careful look. A piece is pinned when moving it exposes something more valuable behind it. If moving the piece would expose its own king to check, it cannot legally make that move. This can make a defended square effectively vulnerable. However, a pinned piece may still capture along the line of the pin or make another move that keeps the king protected. A piece pinned to a queen can legally move, though it may lose the queen. Always inspect the actual legal responses before treating a defender as completely frozen.

The enemy queen is often a defensive target even when you cannot win her. You may threaten something elsewhere that forces her away from the king. You may offer a queen exchange if your remaining pieces can attack effectively, though usually exchanging queens reduces mating danger. You may block her route back with a pawn or rook. Think in terms of access. A queen on the far side of a locked pawn chain may be powerful in principle but unable to reach the threatened area quickly. Distance in chess is measured by usable routes, not just appearance.

Escape squares are another kind of target. A check is far more dangerous when the king has few safe destinations. A bishop can control a diagonal behind the king. A rook can cut off a row. A knight can cover two flight squares while attacking another piece. These restrictions form a mating net, which means an arrangement of control that limits the king's escape. The net must be real. A square occupied by an enemy pawn may become available if that pawn moves or is captured. Recheck the king's options after every exchange.

Here is a simplified picture. Your rook can give check along a straight line, but the enemy king has a safe square one step toward the center. Your bishop could move so that its diagonal controls that escape square. The bishop move may be more threatening than an immediate rook check, provided it is safe and the opponent has no stronger response. The lesson is that controlling the exit can matter more than knocking on the door. A quiet move that removes an escape can create a serious threat without giving check itself.

Do not confuse a net with a trap that only works if the opponent cooperates. The defender might exchange your bishop, block the checking line, create another escape square, or give a forcing check against your own king. Before concluding that you have a mating attack, search for these resources. A good attacker becomes better by practicing defense from the opponent's side. If you can describe how the opponent would like to escape, you can often find the move that makes that escape difficult. If you cannot stop it, adjust the plan honestly.

Targets can be created through pawn contact. A pawn advance may force the opponent to capture, advance, or allow a capture. Each choice changes the shelter differently. If the opponent captures, a file may open. If the opponent advances, a square behind the pawn may become weak. If the opponent leaves the tension, you may choose the best moment to exchange. This is why a well prepared pawn advance can be stronger than repeatedly attacking a defended pawn with pieces. It gives the defender several answers, each with a cost that you can evaluate.

The word prepared matters. Advancing a pawn near the enemy king can leave your own squares weak or block a bishop that was already attacking. Before the move, identify the likely pawn captures and which lines they open. After the move, make sure your pieces can use the resulting position. A pawn that reaches the enemy shelter and simply becomes stuck may be a useful restriction, or it may be an obstacle to your rooks. The value depends on the purpose. A dramatic advance is not automatically progress.

When several targets exist, choose one that your pieces can pressure together. If your bishop aims at one pawn, your rook attacks a different pawn, and your queen threatens a third, the opponent may defend each comfortably. Concentrating on a shared entry point can create a more serious problem. On the other hand, pressure against two separated weaknesses may stretch the defense. The difference is whether the threats cooperate. Ask whether attacking the first target makes the second harder to defend. If the plans have no connection, you may be dividing your own strength.

The easiest target is not always the most important. An undefended side pawn may be available, but capturing it could send your queen far from the king and lose the attack. A well defended pawn near the king may be more valuable because forcing one defender to move opens a mating route. Compare what the capture changes. Does it create access, remove a defender, or merely collect material? Material gain is a legitimate goal, but understand the price in time and coordination. Sometimes taking the pawn is excellent because the attack has already achieved its purpose.

You should also distinguish permanent targets from temporary ones. A permanently weakened square may remain useful for many moves. A loose defender might be vulnerable only before the opponent connects the rooks. A king in the center may become safe after castling. The more temporary the opportunity, the more carefully you should investigate forcing play now. A lasting weakness may allow you to improve your pieces first. This helps you avoid both rushing a sound position and waiting until an opportunity disappears. Timing begins with knowing what the opponent can repair.

Let us turn these ideas into three practice questions. First, the opponent has advanced a pawn beside the king. Your queen can approach the square behind that pawn, but none of your pieces can protect the queen there, and the king would be able to capture her. Is the square a usable attacking target right now?

The answer is no. It may be strategically weak, but your queen cannot occupy it safely under the stated conditions. Look for a way to add support or use a different route. Weakness without access does not justify entry.

Notice what would change the answer. A bishop might reach a diagonal protecting the entry square. A knight might cover it from a stable post. The opponent might move the king farther away while answering another threat. Each change alters the relationship that made entry unsafe. This is how you turn a vague observation into a plan. Rather than saying, that square looks weak, say, that square becomes useful if I can protect the piece that enters. The preparation now has a clear job and a clear test of success.

Second, a defending knight covers the square where your queen wants to enter. You can trade your bishop for that knight, but the opponent would recapture with a pawn and open a line for a rook that also guards the king. Should you assume the exchange helps your attack?

No. You must evaluate the new defender and the newly opened line. Removing one guardian can activate another. The right comparison is between the complete position before the exchange and the complete position afterward. Counting only the removed knight would miss the main defensive improvement.

Third, your rook can give a harmless check that lets the king step toward the center. Your bishop can instead make a safe move that controls that escape square and threatens a stronger rook entry next turn. What makes the bishop move worth considering?

It improves the mating net by taking away the defender's easiest answer. You must still check whether the opponent can exchange the bishop, block the rook, or create an urgent counterthreat. The bishop move is a candidate because it changes the king's choices, not simply because quiet moves are supposed to be clever.

For practice in your own games, pause before a possible attack and describe three things in ordinary language. Describe one pawn or square you want to use. Describe the piece that prevents you from using it. Describe the route by which your own pieces can arrive. If one part is missing, investigate it. This exercise trains you to connect targets with methods. Over time, you will notice the useful details faster: a knight defending too much, a queen too far away, or a pawn advance that leaves a permanent entry point.

You can also review a failed attack by asking which target disappeared. Perhaps the opponent traded the bishop that supported your queen. Perhaps a pawn advance created an escape square. Perhaps you captured the wrong pawn and closed your own line. A failed attack often becomes understandable once you identify that change. The goal is not to memorize an endless list of mating patterns. It is to understand the jobs those patterns require. Pieces must reach the king, support one another, and restrict the replies that would otherwise make the attack harmless.

In your next game, look beyond the king itself. Find the pawn that can be challenged, the square that can be entered, the defender that can be displaced, or the escape route that can be closed. Then connect that target to a realistic plan. A useful target is one that your pieces can exploit and that forces the opponent to make a difficult choice. Once you can explain that relationship aloud, your attacking moves will become easier to choose, easier to calculate, and much less dependent on hope.
