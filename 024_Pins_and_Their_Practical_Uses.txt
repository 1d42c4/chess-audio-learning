Pins and Their Practical Uses

A pin is a relationship between an attacking piece, a piece in front, and something important behind it. A bishop, rook, or queen aims along a line. An enemy piece stands on that line, shielding a more important target. Moving the shielding piece would expose the target behind it. This can restrict the piece's freedom and make it vulnerable to further pressure. Pins are common because chess pieces often share lines while defending one another. Understanding them helps you attack, defend, exchange, and judge whether a piece really can perform its apparent duties.

Imagine a bishop looking along an open diagonal toward the enemy king, with one enemy knight between them. The knight blocks the bishop's line. If the knight moves away and exposes its king to check, that move is illegal. This is an absolute pin: the king is the target behind the pinned piece. The knight may appear to attack useful squares, but its legal moves are restricted by the king's safety. A pin to the king can therefore make a defender unable to capture an ordinary attacking piece that it would otherwise be able to take.

A relative pin has a valuable piece, rather than the king, behind the pinned piece. Suppose a rook attacks a bishop that shields the queen on the same line. The bishop can legally move if doing so does not expose its king, but moving may lose the queen. The restriction is practical rather than absolute. That distinction matters because the bishop may have a checking move, a mating attack, or a capture worth more than the queen behind it. A relative pin creates a cost for moving; it does not remove the right to move.

Do not treat all pinned pieces as completely frozen. A piece pinned along a line may be able to move along that same line while continuing to shield the king. A rook pinned on a file might slide toward the attacker without exposing the king. A bishop pinned on a diagonal might capture the pinning bishop if the path and other conditions allow it. A knight cannot remain on the same straight line through one of its L-shaped moves, so an absolute line pin usually removes its legal moves. Always examine the specific move rather than relying on the label alone.

Capturing the pinning piece can end the problem immediately. If a bishop pins your knight, you may be able to capture the bishop with another piece or pawn. You might also attack it with a pawn and force it to move, though the bishop may retreat while maintaining the pin. Exchanging the attacker can be useful, but the resulting pawn structure and piece balance still matter. A pin is one feature of the position. Removing it at the cost of a much larger weakness may solve the wrong problem.

Moving the target behind the pinned piece is another common defense. If your queen is behind a pinned knight, moving the queen can free the knight. If your king is behind the piece, a legal king move may remove the absolute pin. The destination must be safe, and the move must address any immediate threat. Sometimes moving the king costs castling rights or worsens its shelter, so the cure has a price. Compare that price with the danger of allowing the pin to remain and intensify.

You can also interpose another piece between the attacker and the target, changing the line's structure. A rook may step onto the line and take over the shielding duty. A pawn may advance to block a bishop's diagonal. These defenses require care because the new blocker can become pinned instead. The aim is to create a position where the attack no longer causes serious restriction or material loss. Simply transferring the pin to a more valuable or less protected piece may make the problem worse rather than solve it.

The attacking side often exploits a pin by attacking the pinned piece again. A knight that cannot move away from a bishop's line may be a good target for a pawn advance. If the opponent cannot defend it enough times, it can be won. However, counting attackers and defenders is only a starting point. The pinning piece may be vulnerable, a countercheck may interrupt the sequence, or the pinned piece may move legally along the line. A pin creates a possible tactical opportunity; the additional attack must still work against the best defense.

Pawns are especially useful attackers of pinned pieces because they are relatively inexpensive. A pawn advance can threaten to exchange a pawn for a knight or bishop that cannot retreat. Yet pawn moves can weaken your own king or structure, and the opponent may break the pin before you capture. Ask whether the attack is immediate and whether the pinned piece has a tactical way to escape. A pawn push that looks like it wins a piece can be premature if the pinning bishop can be driven away with a forcing reply.

Another exploitation is to use a square that the pinned piece appears to defend. Suppose a knight is absolutely pinned to its king and would have to expose the king to capture your queen. A queen move onto one of the knight's attacked squares may be legal and tactically safe from that knight, because the knight cannot make the capture. Other defenders still matter. This is an important difference between geometric attacks and legal captures. A pinned defender may look useful in a simple count while being unable to perform the specific defensive move required.

There is a crucial king-safety exception to a beginner's shorthand. A pinned piece is still considered to attack squares for the purpose of deciding where a king may move. You cannot move your king onto a square attacked by an enemy piece merely because that enemy piece is pinned to its own king. The rules treat that square as attacked even when moving the pinned piece would expose its king. Therefore, do not apply the slogan pinned pieces do not attack to king moves. It is an unsafe simplification of a more precise rule.

For ordinary pieces, the practical question is whether the defender can legally and effectively make the capture. For kings, attacked-square rules impose a stricter boundary on legal movement. Keeping those questions separate prevents confusion. A queen may sometimes enter a square controlled only by an absolutely pinned knight, while a king may not enter that same attacked square. You do not need a complicated board example to remember the lesson: evaluate actual captures when counting defenders, and evaluate attacked squares when checking the king's legal destinations.

A relative pin is more vulnerable to counterplay. A knight pinned to the queen may jump with check, giving its owner time to move or exchange the queen later. A bishop may abandon a rook behind it to deliver mate. A pawn may move out of a pin because the resulting attack wins greater material. Whenever your tactic relies on a relative pin, explicitly search for forcing moves by the pinned piece. The piece's apparent obligation to stay is only as strong as the value of what it protects compared with what it can achieve by moving.

Pins can have positional value without winning material immediately. A pinned knight may be unable to challenge a central pawn advance. A pinned pawn may restrict the movement of a neighboring piece. A rook tied to shielding a queen may be unable to contest another file. These restrictions can give you time to develop, gain space, or improve a piece. The pin's value is the useful freedom it creates for your army. If you cannot exploit the restriction before the opponent removes it, the pin may be less important than it looks.

Do not preserve a pin merely because it looks uncomfortable. You may be able to exchange the pinned piece and damage the opponent's pawn structure, or trade the pinning bishop for a strong knight at a favorable moment. Alternatively, keeping the bishop may maintain pressure that is more valuable than the exchange. Compare the positions after the trade and after retaining tension. A pin is a means to an advantage, not an object to admire indefinitely. Its strategic value changes as pieces develop and the king or queen moves away.

The distinction between a pin and a skewer can clarify the pattern. In a pin, the piece in front is constrained because something important stands behind it. In a skewer, the more urgent or valuable target is in front and is attacked, so moving it exposes a target behind. A rook checking a king along a line, with an undefended queen farther behind, illustrates the idea of a skewer. Both tactics use alignment, but the order of the targets changes which piece is expected to move and why.

To recognize possible pins, inspect open lines toward kings, queens, and rooks. Ask whether one enemy piece is the only shield on the line. Then check whether your bishop, rook, or queen can occupy the line safely. A pinning move that places your bishop where a pawn can capture it immediately may fail. A pin that allows the opponent to attack your queen may be too slow. As with other tactical motifs, the pattern suggests a candidate move; the resulting position and replies decide its quality.

Here is the first practice situation. A bishop pins an enemy knight to the enemy king. The knight has no legal move that preserves the king's safety, and no other enemy piece can capture your attacking pawn. Your pawn can advance to attack the knight, while the opponent has no countercheck or way to break the pin in time. Why might the pawn advance win material? Consider the knight's inability to retreat and the defender's lack of an alternative way to remove the pressure.

The pawn threatens to capture a more valuable piece that cannot legally move away, and the stated conditions rule out the usual ways to break the pin or remove the pawn. The advance therefore creates a concrete material threat. In a real position, you must establish those conditions carefully. Check whether the bishop can be exchanged, whether the king can move to unpin the knight, and whether an intermediate check changes the timing. The phrase attack the pinned piece is a useful idea only when the restriction survives long enough to be exploited.

The second practice situation concerns a relative pin. Your rook attacks an enemy bishop that shields the enemy queen. You assume the bishop cannot move. However, the bishop has a legal move that gives check and creates a mating threat, forcing your king to respond while the queen can be saved afterward. Is the bishop absolutely immobilized by the pin? Think about the difference between an illegal move and a move that carries a material cost.

It is not absolutely immobilized. The queen behind the bishop creates a relative pin, so the bishop may move legally when the resulting consequences justify it. The checking move can change the order of events and prevent you from taking advantage of the exposed queen immediately. You must calculate the continuation rather than assuming the bishop remains in place. This example explains why forcing moves by relatively pinned pieces deserve special attention. A valuable target behind a piece creates pressure, but it does not cancel that piece's tactical resources.

The third practice situation is a rules question. An enemy knight is pinned to its king and cannot legally move away from the pin. Your king would like to step onto a square that the knight attacks. No other enemy piece attacks that square. Is the king move legal simply because the knight could not move to capture the king without exposing its own king? Separate the knight's legal moves from the rules governing attacked squares before answering.

The king move is not legal. The pinned knight still attacks its normal squares for the purpose of king safety. A king may not enter one of those squares merely because the knight is pinned. This is why the common shorthand about pinned pieces needs qualification. It can help explain why a pinned defender cannot capture an ordinary piece in a tactical sequence, but it must not be used to authorize a king move into an attacked square. King safety follows the attacked-square rule, not an imagined permission to trade kings.

For training, find a position with a pin and explain three things in words: what piece creates the pin, what piece is restricted, and what target stands behind it. Then decide whether the pin is absolute or relative. Finally, name at least two ways the defender might try to escape it. This exercise makes the relationship concrete without requiring coordinates. If you cannot identify the target behind the piece or the line that connects them, you may be describing ordinary pressure rather than an actual pin.

When reviewing your games, ask whether you overestimated or underestimated a pin. Perhaps you assumed a knight could defend a pawn when it could not move legally. Perhaps you treated a bishop as frozen even though it had a forcing check. Perhaps you spent several moves maintaining a pin while the opponent improved elsewhere. These are different mistakes. The first concerns legal defensive ability, the second tactical resources, and the third strategic value. Naming the category helps you practice the part of pin understanding that needs attention.

You can prevent many pins through sensible piece placement and development. Avoid placing your queen behind an undefended minor piece on an open enemy line without a reason. Give your king a safe position and connect your pieces so one blocker does not carry every defensive duty. Yet do not fear every alignment. Some pins are harmless because you can exchange the attacker or move the target at any time. Prevention should improve the position, not force awkward moves to avoid a restriction the opponent cannot actually exploit.

Pins turn alignment into restriction. Use them to limit a defender, attack a piece that cannot retreat, or gain time for a useful plan. Defend by removing the attacker, moving the target, changing the line, or finding a stronger forcing resource. Always distinguish an absolute pin from a relative one, and remember the special attacked-square rule for kings. A pin becomes valuable when its specific restriction matters to the next stage of play. Understanding that restriction precisely will serve you better than treating every pinned piece as helpless or every pin as a win.
