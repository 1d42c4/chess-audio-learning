Discovered Attacks

Your knight moves to attack a rook. At the same time, the knight's departure opens your bishop's diagonal toward the enemy queen. One move has created two attacks with two different pieces. This is the power of a discovered attack: moving one unit releases an attack from another unit behind it. The piece that moves may be important mainly because of what it uncovers. Learning to see this relationship helps you find combinations and notice threats that are easy to miss when you watch only the destination.

A discovered attack requires a sliding piece and a line that is currently blocked. The sliding piece is a rook, bishop, or queen. A friendly piece or pawn stands between that attacker and an enemy target. When the friendly blocker moves out of the way, the line opens and the attack reaches the target. The moving unit can be a knight, bishop, rook, queen, pawn, or even the king if the move is legal. What matters is the newly opened line, not the identity of the blocker.

The basic ingredients are the rear attacker, the movable blocker, and the enemy target. Trace them in that order along a horizontal row, a vertical column called a file, or a diagonal. There must be no additional blocker preventing the attack after the moving piece leaves. A rook behind a knight may appear ready to attack an enemy queen, but a pawn farther along the file can still obstruct it. See the whole path. A promising alignment becomes a real discovered attack only when the departure actually creates a clear line to the target.

The discovered attack is not automatically a double attack. If the blocker moves to a harmless square and the rear piece attacks a queen, only one new threat may matter. The opponent can often answer it normally. The tactic becomes more powerful when the moving unit creates another serious threat at the same time. That second threat might be a capture, checkmate, promotion, or a direct attack on a valuable piece. The opponent may then be unable to meet both demands with one move.

Consider a conceptual arrangement with your bishop behind your knight on a diagonal leading toward the enemy queen. The knight can move away and attack a rook. After that move, the bishop attacks the queen while the knight attacks the rook. The opponent may have to save the queen and allow the rook to be captured. But first check whether the queen can capture the knight, whether the rook can escape with check, or whether one move can meet both attacks. The two-threat description is a starting point for calculation, not proof of a win.

The value of the uncovered target affects how much freedom the blocker gains. An attack on a queen is usually more urgent than an attack on a well defended pawn. If the rear piece merely attacks something the opponent can safely ignore, your moving piece may not have time to execute its second threat. Compare the consequences of both attacks. A discovered attack is useful when the opponent faces a real choice or loses a meaningful tempo, meaning a move of useful time, not simply when two arrows could be drawn on the board.

The moving piece can sometimes capture material that would ordinarily be unsafe to take. A knight may capture a pawn while uncovering an attack on the queen. The opponent might be unable to capture the knight because saving the queen is more important. This does not make the knight immune. The queen may escape with a counterthreat, or another piece may capture the knight while also blocking the bishop's line. Calculate acceptance of the apparent sacrifice and the opponent's strongest alternative. Temporary freedom must be justified by concrete urgency.

If the uncovered line attacks the enemy king, the move creates a discovered check. This is a particularly forcing form because the opponent must make the king safe. The moving piece may use that tempo to capture material, attack a queen, or occupy an important square. Still, the defender can answer a discovered check by any legal method that removes it, including capturing the rear attacker or blocking its line. Do not assume the king must move unless the actual position leaves no other legal response.

If both the moving piece and the revealed piece check the king, the result is double check. The king must move in response because a non-king move cannot remove both checking attacks at once. This gives the moving piece unusual freedom to create a forcing geometry. However, double check does not guarantee checkmate or even an advantage. The king may capture one checking piece on a square safe from the other or escape to a secure square. Inspect the resulting king moves carefully before evaluating the tactic.

A discovered attack can also clear a route for a pawn. A pawn may capture diagonally away from a bishop's line and reveal an attack while approaching promotion. In other positions, a pawn's straight advance releases a rook or queen along a row. Pawn moves have permanent structural effects, so assess what the pawn stops defending. The discovered threat may gain time, but the new pawn position remains after the tactic ends. A temporary attack should be weighed against lasting weaknesses if the opponent can meet it without a material loss.

The blocker does not have unlimited freedom. It must make a legal move, and its departure must not expose its own king to check. A knight might appear able to uncover your bishop while attacking a queen, but it may also be pinned along an enemy rook's file. Moving it away could be illegal. A king that serves as the blocker must move to a safe square. Always inspect both kings' lines. A discovered attack for you can coincide with an exposed attack against you, and your own king's safety remains mandatory.

Sometimes moving the blocker along the same line fails to uncover anything. If your rook stands behind your queen on a file and the queen moves farther along that same file, she may still block the rook. The queen has moved, but she remains between the rear attacker and target. Her own new threat may still be useful, but it should not be confused with releasing the rook behind her. Verify the final arrangement instead of assuming that any move by the blocker releases the attack. The important event is clearing the line completely.

The blocker may also land where it interferes with your intended continuation. A knight move can reveal an attack on the queen but occupy the square your bishop needed for a later capture. A pawn capture can uncover one line while closing another. A rook may move out of a diagonal and accidentally block the queen's checking row. After imagining the discovered attack, evaluate the moving piece's destination as carefully as its departure. Coordination means the two pieces' new jobs work together rather than obstruct one another.

Look for discovered attacks when your sliding pieces sit behind active friendly pieces. A bishop behind a central knight often has the potential to gain a diagonal when the knight moves. A rook behind a pawn can become active when the pawn captures away from the file. A queen behind a bishop may gain a line toward the king after a forcing bishop move. These arrangements are not always tactical immediately. They are latent possibilities that can influence where the opponent safely places valuable pieces.

You can prepare a discovered attack by bringing the rear piece onto the appropriate line. The opponent may then need to move the target or interfere with the setup. This can gain a useful tempo even if no material is won. But preparing the battery, which is an arrangement of pieces working along a common line, takes time. Ask whether the opponent can attack your blocker, exchange the rear piece, or create a stronger threat before you finish. A planned discovery needs a workable move order and a reason the target cannot simply leave.

Luring the target onto the line is another method. You may exchange a piece so that an enemy queen is tempted to recapture on the dangerous diagonal. You may give check that forces a king onto the line your blocker currently interrupts. These ideas combine discovered attacks with deflection or attraction, meaning moves that redirect an enemy piece. The opponent's alternatives are crucial. If they can recapture differently or choose another king square, the setup may fail. Calculate the forcing mechanism rather than assuming they will occupy the square your combination requires.

Defense begins by noticing the line behind the moving piece. After every opponent move, ask what that piece had been blocking. If a knight jumps toward your rook, also inspect the bishop or queen that might now attack through its old square. A dangerous discovery often succeeds because the defender reacts to the visible attack and misses the quieter one. The opposite can happen too: you move the queen away from the revealed bishop and overlook the knight's fork. Name both threats before deciding how to respond.

When a discovered attack is threatened but not yet played, you can move the target off the line, exchange the blocker or rear attacker, add a useful blocker, or create a forcing threat that prevents the setup. Sometimes attacking the blocker makes the discovery stronger rather than weaker, because it gives the piece a useful reason to move. Calculate the opponent's departure options before chasing it. A pawn attack on a knight is not automatically a gain of time if the knight can leave with check and uncover an attack on your queen.

After the discovery occurs, look for a move that answers both threats. A queen move may escape the bishop's line while capturing the knight that created the second attack. A rook move placed between the checking piece and king may block the revealed check and defend the other target. A checking capture may force a favorable exchange before the opponent collects material. Such resources depend on the exact geometry. They are worth searching for because the natural response to a double attack is often to assume one target must fall without checking whether the duties can be combined.

Your first practice question concerns a knight in front of your bishop. The enemy queen lies farther along the same diagonal. You can move the knight away, but another pawn would still remain between the bishop and queen. Have you created a discovered attack on the queen? What feature of the resulting line decides the answer? Trace from the bishop toward the queen and stop at the first remaining piece.

You have not created a direct attack on the queen through the remaining pawn. The knight's departure may improve the bishop's scope, but the pawn still blocks the path. You would need another move or exchange to remove that obstacle, and the opponent would have a chance to respond. This example separates a useful line-opening idea from an immediate tactical attack. A discovery requires the rear piece's legal movement to reach the target after the blocker leaves, with no additional piece still obstructing the line.

Your second practice question involves two attacks. Your knight moves away from a rook's file and attacks an enemy bishop. The rook now attacks the enemy queen. The queen can move off the file and capture your knight safely in the same move. Does the existence of two attacks guarantee that you win the bishop? Consider what the queen's move accomplishes and which attacking piece remains afterward.

The queen may answer both threats by escaping the rook's attack and removing the knight that threatened the bishop. If the capture is legal and safe, the apparent double attack does not win the bishop as planned and may lose your knight. You must calculate that combined defensive response. There may be another knight destination that creates a stronger discovery, but the original move is not justified by the label alone. Count the actual surviving threats after the opponent's best reply, not just the threats present immediately after your move.

Your third practice question concerns a discovered check. Your bishop moves away from a rook's line, revealing a rook check against the enemy king. The bishop also attacks a queen. The opponent has a legal rook move that blocks the checking line and protects the queen. Must the king move simply because the check was discovered? What should you examine about the interposition?

The king does not have to move if another legal move removes the check. Examine whether the interposing rook can be captured profitably, whether the queen remains protected, and whether your bishop has another useful continuation. The discovered check may still produce a gain, but its forcing nature does not eliminate all blocks or captures. Only double check has the specific requirement that the king move. Here you must assess the defender's actual resource and the position that results after the rook interrupts the line.

For optional practice later, take a position from your own games and identify a sliding piece with a friendly blocker in front of it. Ask what target lies beyond the blocker and where the blocker could move with a useful threat. Test the opponent's strongest answer. If no tactic works, identify the missing ingredient: a blocked line, an unimportant target, an unsafe destination, or a combined defense. This exercise builds pattern recognition while teaching you to distinguish real discoveries from arrangements that merely look promising.

Review a missed discovered attack from both perspectives. As the attacker, ask whether you noticed the rear piece's line before choosing the blocker's move. As the defender, ask whether you checked the square the moving piece left behind. These are complementary habits. One helps you coordinate two threats in a single turn. The other helps you avoid reacting to only half of an opponent's move. Both depend on seeing the board as changing relationships among pieces rather than as isolated pieces moving one at a time.

A discovered attack releases a hidden line by moving its blocker. Its strength comes from the urgency of the revealed threat and the useful work the moving piece can do at the same time. Verify the clear path, the legality of the departure, and the safety of the destination. Then inspect the opponent's captures, blocks, counterchecks, and combined defenses. Once you understand those conditions, the pattern becomes both easier to find and harder to fall for, because you know which two pieces are cooperating and what their move actually demands.
