Creating a Second Target During Conversion

You have a better position and a clear target. An enemy pawn is weak, your pieces attack it, and the defender has been forced into passivity. Yet nothing seems to happen. Every additional attack is met by another defender, and the pawn remains secure. This is a common moment in converting an advantage. The answer may be to create a second target, so that the defender must divide attention between two problems instead of arranging the entire army around one.

The principle of two weaknesses is a way to understand this process. A single weakness can often be defended because all available pieces can cooperate around it. A second weakness, especially far away, may stretch those pieces beyond their capacity. The stronger side can then switch pressure, force a concession, or penetrate with the king. The principle is a guide to planning, not a rule that exactly two weak pawns guarantee a win. The targets must be accessible, the attackers must be coordinated, and the opponent's counterplay must remain controlled.

A target does not have to be a pawn. It can be an entry square, an exposed king, a restricted piece, or a passed pawn that forces the defender to stay in one area. Suppose the opponent's rook must defend a weak pawn while their king must stop your passer. Those are two separate duties even if you are attacking only one enemy pawn. Think in terms of problems the defender must solve. The useful question is whether one defensive arrangement can comfortably meet all your threats.

Before seeking a second target, confirm that the first one is stable. If the weak pawn can advance safely as soon as you look away, you may lose your original source of pressure. You might need to blockade it, control a supporting pawn break, or keep one attacker in place. The aim is to preserve enough pressure that the defender cannot freely redeploy. A second target is most effective when it is added to an existing problem. If creating it requires abandoning the first entirely, the defense may simply move across and remain comfortable.

This does not mean every attacker must stay fixed. One piece may maintain the original pressure while another transfers. A rook can continue attacking a backward pawn along a half open file. Your king can then walk toward the opposite wing, or your queen can switch diagonals. A half open file is a straight lane without your pawn but with an enemy pawn remaining in it. Because the rook keeps the first target under observation, the defender cannot ignore it while you develop the new threat elsewhere.

Distance matters because pieces have different travel speeds. A rook or queen can switch wings quickly along an open line. A knight may need several moves. A king travels one square at a time and can be delayed by pawn barriers. A bishop moves quickly but only on one color and only along unobstructed diagonals. When you create a second target, ask which defender will have to respond and how long the journey takes. Your plan becomes stronger when your attacking pieces can change direction more easily than the defending pieces.

Imagine a simplified rook ending in which the enemy rook is tied to a weak pawn on one wing. Your rook keeps pressure on that pawn, and your king has a safe route toward the other wing. If your king can attack a second pawn there, the defender may need to choose between losing the first pawn and allowing the king to enter. This is a conceptual plan rather than a fully specified forced win. The exact king distances, checks, and pawn advances determine whether it can succeed in a real position.

Creating a weakness often involves a pawn advance. You may push a pawn to provoke an enemy pawn move, then attack the squares or pawns that move leaves behind. This is useful but irreversible. Pawns cannot retreat, and your own advance may create a target too. Before pushing, compare the new weaknesses on both sides. If you gain a target but expose your king or give the enemy rook an open file, the trade may be unfavorable. A second weakness should increase your practical advantage, not merely make the position more complicated.

A minority attack is one example of creating a target with pawns. A smaller pawn group advances against a larger group, aiming to exchange pawns and leave a backward or isolated pawn. You do not need to memorize an opening to understand the idea. The attacking pawns are trying to change the structure, not necessarily promote. The pieces must support the operation, and the defender may respond with central counterplay. In a conversion, the attack is strongest when the opponent is already tied down and cannot use the time to create a more urgent threat.

Sometimes a target is created by forcing a piece into an awkward role. If an enemy bishop must defend a pawn, it may lose control of an entry square. Your king can approach that square, creating a second problem without any pawn move. If the bishop leaves, the pawn falls. If it stays, the king penetrates. This is a form of overloading: one defender has two duties that conflict. The conflict must be concrete. A bishop may defend both points along the same diagonal, in which case you have not yet stretched it enough.

The color of a bishop's squares can help you choose the second target. A bishop cannot attack or defend squares of the opposite color directly. If the first weakness is securely covered by the bishop, your king or rook may seek an entry on the other color. In opposite colored bishop endings, however, the defender may build a blockade your bishop cannot challenge. Creating a second front can help, but the drawing resources are strong. Do not assume that a distant pawn automatically breaks the blockade. King access and pawn placement still decide the result.

In positions with queens, the enemy king can become the second target. Pressure against a weak pawn may tie down a rook or bishop that would otherwise defend the king. Your queen can then switch toward the king with a real threat. Conversely, an attack on the king may force a pawn advance that creates a new weakness elsewhere. Be careful about your own king during such switches. The defender may gain checks when your queen leaves its previous square. A second target is useful only if you have time to pursue it.

The order of operations is important. You may need to improve your worst piece, secure the king, or prevent a pawn break before changing wings. If you transfer the queen too early, the opponent may free the original weakness and become active. If you wait too long, the defender may reorganize so that both targets are covered. Identify the moment when the first target is sufficiently fixed and your transferring piece has a safe route. Preparation should lead to a concrete transition, not to endless maneuvering without a new threat.

One practical method is to ask the defender's question. Which move would they like to make if the first weakness did not exist? Perhaps their rook would become active, or their king would cross toward the center. Keep enough pressure to prevent that move. Then ask which part of the board has the fewest defenders. That area may offer your second target. This approach links restriction and creation. You are not choosing a distant pawn at random; you are choosing a problem the defender cannot meet without relaxing the first duty.

You should also count the moves required to create the target. If you need four quiet moves and the opponent needs two to generate a passed pawn, your plan may be too slow. Compare timelines. The stronger side does not receive unlimited turns simply because the position looks better. A second-target plan is often strongest in a stable position where the opponent's useful pawn breaks have been controlled. In a sharp position, a direct tactical action or defensive move may take priority over a long maneuver.

There is a danger of mistaking visible separation for genuine independence. Two weak pawns may sit far apart, yet a rook can defend both from the same row. Two entry squares may be covered by one bishop along a single diagonal. In those cases, moving pressure between them may not force a concession. Study the defender's arrangement. Can one piece answer both threats without moving, or move between them without losing time? You may need to alter the structure, displace that defender, or create a threat of a different type.

A strong second target often demands a different response from the first. A rook defending a pawn may be able to protect another pawn on the same line, but it cannot necessarily stop a king invasion on the opposite side. A bishop holding a passed pawn may be unable to answer a rook entering behind its king. Combining different kinds of threats can create a more difficult defensive problem than attacking two similar pawns. The principle concerns conflicting duties, not a literal requirement to find two identical weaknesses.

Once the defender makes a concession, reassess before continuing. They may abandon the first pawn to activate their rook, or exchange into an ending they believe is drawn. Capturing the pawn may now be correct, but verify the resulting activity. Sometimes the concession opens a direct invasion that is stronger than the original plan. Do not remain attached to creating the second target after the defense has already changed. The purpose was to force a favorable transformation. When that transformation appears, evaluate and use it.

Consider a conceptual example with a weak central pawn and a vulnerable king shelter. Your rook attacks the pawn, forcing a bishop to defend it. Your queen shifts toward the king, and the defender moves a pawn to stop an immediate threat. That pawn move leaves a new square for your knight. You might now bring the knight in rather than continue attacking the king directly. The second target has produced a third useful feature, but the same discipline applies: check counterplay, improve coordination, and choose the clearest route forward.

Your first practice question is about maintaining pressure. You have a rook attacking a weak pawn and a king that can approach the opposite wing. If the rook leaves, the pawn can advance and the defender becomes active. If the rook stays, the king can move safely toward a second pawn. Which piece should usually do the transferring work in this description, and why? Think about preserving the first problem while creating the next one.

The king should usually be the transferring piece, while the rook maintains the restriction. This keeps the defender tied to the original pawn as the king creates pressure elsewhere. You still check whether the king's route is safe and whether enemy checks or pawn breaks can interrupt it. The description suggests a division of labor: one unit fixes the first target, another creates the second. Moving the rook away would remove the original problem and might allow the defender to meet the new one with full freedom.

Your second practice question concerns a pawn advance. You can push a flank pawn to create a second weakness in the enemy structure, but the push also opens a diagonal toward your own king. The opponent's queen and bishop are active. Should you assume the plan is good because two weaknesses are better than one? What must be compared before making the irreversible move?

You must compare the new target with the immediate danger to your king. If opening the diagonal gives the opponent forcing checks or a mating attack, the second weakness may be irrelevant. Look for preparation that closes the diagonal, exchanges an attacker, or moves the king to safety. You may also choose a different way to create pressure, such as improving a piece or attacking an entry square. The principle does not justify every pawn advance. A useful target must be created at a cost your position can afford.

Your third practice question is about two pawns that seem weak. They are far apart, but the defending rook can protect both along the same horizontal row. Your rook can attack either pawn but cannot safely enter behind them. Have you necessarily created a winning two-target situation? What kind of change would make the defender's task more difficult?

You have not necessarily created enough strain because one rook can meet both duties from the same arrangement. Look for a king invasion, a pawn break that changes the row, or a way to force the defending rook away. A threat of a different type may create the conflict that two similar pawn attacks do not. You might also improve your rook's entry route before switching targets. The important test is whether the defender must make a concession, not whether the weaknesses are physically separated on the board.

For training, examine one of your games where pressure stalled against a single pawn. Identify the piece that maintained that pressure and ask which other piece could have changed the area of play. Search for a second pawn, an entry square, or a king-safety weakness. Then analyze the defender's best active response. This exercise helps distinguish a realistic second front from a pleasant fantasy. The best plan is one that preserves the first restriction, creates a real new duty, and arrives before the defender frees the position.

A second exercise is to play a simplified position from both sides. As the attacker, try to keep one weakness fixed while improving another piece. As the defender, try to exchange the fixed pawn, activate a rook, or place one piece where it covers both problems. You will learn what makes a defensive arrangement resilient. That knowledge improves your attacking technique because you stop expecting pressure alone to win. You begin looking for the exact change that makes the defender's duties incompatible.

Creating a second target is a way to turn a static advantage into a sequence of difficult choices. Keep the first weakness under control, use the mobility of your pieces, and seek a new problem that cannot be handled by the same comfortable defense. Compare the time and cost of your plan with the opponent's counterplay. When a concession appears, reassess and choose the strongest continuation. The second target matters because it changes what the defender must do, giving your advantage a practical route toward a win.
