Choosing Candidate Moves

When it is your turn in chess, you do not need to study every legal move with equal attention. You need a small set of moves that deserve serious consideration. These are candidate moves. A candidate is not a promise or a favorite. It is a possibility with a reason behind it. Learning to choose candidates helps you avoid two opposite problems: playing the first attractive move without comparison, and thinking about so many moves that you never examine any of them properly.

The first step is to understand the immediate situation. What changed on the opponent's last move? Is your king in danger? Is a piece attacked? Did a line open, a defender move, or a pawn become weak? These facts determine what kinds of candidate are relevant. If you face a direct mating threat, a lovely long-term plan may not belong in your shortlist at all. If the position is quiet, you can give more attention to improving pieces and preparing changes in the pawn structure.

Separate urgent obligations from desirable improvements. An obligation is something you must address unless you have a stronger concrete resource. Saving a queen, answering check, or preventing a forced material loss can be obligations. Improving an inactive bishop or gaining space is generally a desirable improvement. Sometimes one move does both: a rook retreats from attack onto an open line. Such moves often make good candidates because they meet the immediate demand while improving the position. Look for this overlap before choosing a purely passive defense.

After checking the immediate danger, scan purposeful checks, captures, and serious threats. Forcing moves can change the position so quickly that they deserve early attention. A check that wins a defender, a capture that opens the enemy king, or a quiet threat against two targets may be more important than ordinary development. But do not fill the entire shortlist with forcing moves simply because they are easy to notice. A move can give check and still be poor. A quiet move can be the strongest answer.

To find quiet candidates, ask what your position needs. Perhaps a knight lacks a useful route. Perhaps your rooks are disconnected because the queen occupies their line. Perhaps your king needs a safe escape square. Perhaps a central pawn advance will free a bishop, but first you must protect the pawn behind it. Each diagnosis suggests a kind of move. The candidate should come from a specific job, not from a vague desire to do something active. You are connecting an observed problem to a possible solution.

Your opponent's plan also produces candidates. If a knight is heading toward a protected central square, you might control that square with a pawn, exchange the knight, or create a more urgent threat elsewhere. If a rook is about to enter your position, you might contest the file, cover the entry square, or close the line. These alternatives defend in different ways. Good candidate generation includes prevention, exchange, and counterplay, rather than assuming that the only response to pressure is adding another defender to the target.

There is no magic number of candidates that fits every position. In a forced situation, you may have only one reasonable move. In a rich middlegame, several alternatives can deserve attention. For ordinary practical thinking, a shortlist of two or three serious possibilities is often manageable. The point is not to obey a quota. The point is to consider meaningful alternatives without spreading your calculation too thinly. If every move you name performs the same job in nearly the same way, your shortlist may lack a useful comparison.

Imagine a simplified position where your bishop is blocked by your own central pawns. You could move the bishop toward a longer diagonal, advance a pawn to open its current diagonal, or exchange it for an active enemy knight. These are three genuinely different candidates. The first improves its route, the second changes the structure, and the third removes the problem through an exchange. Comparing them teaches you more than considering three nearly identical bishop retreats. Each alternative asks a different question about what the position needs.

Give each candidate a short purpose statement. My knight move defends the pawn and prepares to challenge the enemy bishop. My rook move contests the open file before the opponent doubles rooks. My pawn advance opens a line while the enemy king is still central. If you cannot explain a move's purpose clearly, investigate whether it belongs on the list. There are subtle useful moves whose value takes work to understand, but a beginner should be especially wary of moves chosen only because they look normal.

Include the cost in that statement when it is obvious. A pawn advance gains space but weakens a neighboring square. A bishop exchange removes an attacker but gives up your best minor piece. A rook lift helps an attack but leaves the back line less protected. Stating the tradeoff does not disqualify the candidate. It tells you what the next stage of analysis must justify, and prevents the attractive part of the move from hiding its obligations.

Then test each candidate against the opponent's strongest plausible reply. This first test is often brief. Does the move leave a piece undefended? Does it allow a check, a fork, or a dangerous pawn break? Does the opponent simply exchange the piece you hoped to improve? A candidate that fails an obvious tactical test can leave the shortlist immediately. You do not need to spend equal time on every idea. Early rejection of unsound moves creates more time for candidates that survive serious resistance.

Do not confuse an opponent's inconvenient reply with a refutation. A move can remain best even if the opponent has a good response. Suppose your rook enters an open file, and the opponent contests it with another rook. That may be normal resistance, not proof that entering the file was wrong. Compare the resulting positions. A candidate is refuted when its consequences make it unacceptable relative to your alternatives, not merely when the opponent refuses to cooperate. Chess rarely offers a useful move that the other player cannot challenge.

You may discover a new candidate while calculating an old one. This is healthy. Perhaps a pawn break fails because your rook is unprotected, suggesting a rook move that prepares the break. Perhaps a knight jump works only after a defender is exchanged, suggesting that exchange first. Candidate generation and calculation can interact. Do not rigidly freeze the shortlist before understanding the position. At the same time, avoid abandoning every line at its first difficulty to chase another attractive idea. Finish enough analysis to know what you learned.

A useful distinction is between a direct move and a preparatory move. The direct move attempts the desired action now. The preparatory move improves the conditions for doing it later. If your intended attack has a tactical flaw, preparation may solve it. But preparation costs a turn, giving the opponent a chance to defend or create counterplay. Include both candidates when timing is important. Ask whether the direct action works already and whether the opponent can prevent it if you wait. Preparation is valuable only when its benefit exceeds its delay.

Another distinction is between changing the position and maintaining flexibility. A pawn advance or exchange often commits you to a new structure. A careful piece improvement may preserve several plans. When neither side has an urgent threat, the flexible move can be attractive because it waits for more information. But flexibility is not the same as aimlessness. Your piece must actually improve, and waiting must not surrender a limited opportunity. A strong shortlist often includes both a useful commitment and a useful way to postpone that commitment.

The character of the position guides the categories you search. In an open position, king safety, development, and active lines may generate the best candidates. In a locked pawn structure, piece routes and well-prepared pawn breaks may matter more. In an endgame, king activity, passed pawns, and favorable exchanges rise in importance. These are starting points, not guarantees. A quiet endgame can still contain an immediate fork, and a closed middlegame can still have a direct attack. Tactical safety remains part of every candidate's evaluation.

Avoid choosing candidates only from the piece your opponent just attacked. If your bishop is threatened, moving it is natural, but you might also capture the attacking piece, block its attack, defend the bishop, or create a stronger threat. Some options may fail immediately; others may save time. This wider search is particularly useful against pawn attacks, where an apparently forced retreat can distract you from a central break. The attacked piece identifies the problem, but it does not dictate the only possible kind of solution.

Likewise, do not assume the best candidate moves your strongest piece. A well-placed knight may already be doing everything you need, while an inactive rook needs help. Moving the knight could surrender its influence for a superficial threat. Asking which piece has the least useful job often generates a practical improvement. Still, an inactive piece may be tied to a necessary defensive duty, so moving it requires care. The question is not which piece looks worst, but which useful improvement is available without creating a larger problem.

The first practice situation begins with your queen attacked by an enemy rook. You identify three legal queen retreats. You also notice that your bishop can capture the attacking rook safely, leaving your queen protected and preserving an active position. Should the candidate list contain only queen moves because the queen is the piece under attack? Think about different ways to solve the immediate problem before selecting the move that will do it.

No. Capturing the attacker belongs on the list because it addresses the threat through a different mechanism. You should verify that the bishop can capture legally and that no stronger reply makes the capture bad. If it survives those checks, it may be better than retreating the queen. The lesson is to generate candidates by the problem they solve, not by the identity of the threatened piece. Retreating, capturing, blocking, defending, and counterattacking can all deserve consideration when the position allows them.

For the second practice situation, suppose you want to open the center because the enemy king has not reached safety. An immediate pawn break loses a pawn without creating usable lines, since your rook is still inactive. Moving that rook to the center would support the break, but the opponent could then secure the king on the next turn. Should you automatically choose the preparatory rook move because the immediate break fails? Consider what happens to the original opportunity during the preparation.

You should not choose it automatically. The rook move may still be useful development, but the attacking opportunity may disappear before the break becomes effective. You need to reassess its purpose and compare it with other candidates. Perhaps there is another way to open the position immediately, or perhaps no sound attack exists and normal improvement is best. The lesson is that a failed direct action does not prove that preparation will make the same plan successful. The opponent also gets a turn while you prepare.

The third practice situation is quieter. Your knight is already secure in the center, and moving it would create a one-move attack that the opponent can easily answer. Your other rook is inactive and can join an open file without tactical risk. You initially consider only two knight moves because the knight looks powerful. What important candidate have you omitted? Give the whole army a job review before deciding which piece should move.

The rook improvement deserves serious consideration. Your central knight may be most useful by staying where it is, while the rook has a clear opportunity to contribute. You should still verify that the open file offers useful entry or pressure and that the rook move does not abandon an essential defense. Under the stated assumptions, it is a meaningful candidate with a different purpose from the knight attacks. The broader lesson is to search for the position's missing contribution, rather than repeatedly moving the piece that already performs well.

You can train candidate selection without calculating very long variations. Pause at a position from a game and name two or three plausible moves in ordinary language. Explain what each accomplishes and what kind of reply would worry you. Then compare your shortlist with a stronger player's explanation or an analysis tool. If the best move never entered your list, ask which feature you overlooked. Was it an undefended piece, an inactive rook, a weak square, or the opponent's next threat? This identifies a recognition problem rather than merely a calculation problem.

If the best move was on your shortlist but you rejected it incorrectly, your training need is different. You may have miscalculated a reply, misunderstood an exchange, or evaluated the resulting position poorly. Distinguishing these errors matters. Searching more broadly will not automatically fix inaccurate calculation, and calculating longer will not help if you never consider the relevant move. Good postgame review asks both whether you found the right ideas and whether you judged those ideas correctly. Candidate selection is one part of a complete thinking process.

During a game, avoid endlessly restarting the search because no move feels perfect. Once you have identified reasonable candidates, tested the main dangers, and compared the resulting positions, make a decision. More time is useful when it resolves a concrete uncertainty. It is less useful when you repeatedly rehearse the same facts. If a critical tactical question remains, investigate it. If the remaining difference is a small strategic preference, choose the sound move whose plan you understand and preserve time for future decisions.

There are positions where candidate selection must be especially broad. Unexpected sacrifices, defensive resources, and unusual retreats can be easy to miss because they conflict with familiar patterns. If every normal move fails, deliberately ask about moves that change the problem: a countercheck, an exchange sacrifice, a king move, or a retreat that defends several points. These possibilities are not automatically good. They deserve attention because the ordinary shortlist has proved inadequate. Difficulty is a signal to widen the search with purpose, not to guess randomly.

Your candidate list should therefore remain both selective and open. Selective means every move has a concrete reason to be considered. Open means new evidence can add a better idea or remove an old favorite. Begin with the opponent's threat, scan meaningful forcing moves, and then consider what your position needs. Include different ways to solve the problem when possible. Test the candidates against real resistance and choose from the positions they produce. You are not trying to think about every move; you are learning to give the right moves a fair hearing.
