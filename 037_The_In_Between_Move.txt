The In Between Move

An opponent captures your bishop, and your hand almost reaches for the piece that can recapture. Before moving, you notice a check. If the opponent answers it, their queen becomes vulnerable, and only afterward do you recover the bishop. That interruption is the idea of an in between move, also called an intermediate move. This lesson explains when to look for one, how to calculate it, and how to avoid the automatic replies that let the opponent insert a stronger threat into your sequence.

An in between move changes the expected order of events by playing something more urgent before the natural response. The natural response may be a recapture, a retreat from attack, or a move stopping a threat. The intermediate action might be a check, a capture, or a serious threat elsewhere. Its purpose is to improve the circumstances in which you eventually make the expected move, or to show that the expected move is no longer necessary.

The key word is urgent. You are leaving some existing problem unresolved, so your new move must justify the delay. A check often does this because the opponent must remove the attack on the king. A threat to win the queen or deliver mate can also change priorities. A casual attack on a pawn usually does not justify ignoring a threatened queen. Compare the consequences of both sides continuing rather than assuming your new threat automatically takes precedence.

You must still obey the rules of check. If your king is currently in check, your move must remove that check. You cannot ignore it to attack the enemy queen or give an unrelated check. A move can be both a valid response to check and a powerful intermediate action, such as capturing the checking piece while creating another threat. But the king's safety requirement comes first. Tactical creativity operates inside legal moves, not as an exception to them.

The most common moment to search is after a capture. Captures create a strong expectation of immediate recapture, and that expectation can hide alternatives. The capturing piece may have abandoned a defensive job, opened a line, or exposed a more valuable target. Before restoring the material balance, inspect checks and forcing captures for both sides. Sometimes you can gain a tempo, improve the recapture, or win additional material by using the new vulnerability first.

Imagine a simplified position where the opponent captures one of your minor pieces, meaning a bishop or knight. You can recapture the attacker, but you also have a queen check that would force the enemy king away from a rook it protects. After the king moves, you might win the rook or recover your piece under better conditions. This is a useful intermediate move idea. The exact result depends on every legal answer to the check and on whether your delayed recapture remains available.

The opponent may use the reply to remove your original opportunity. Their king move might attack your queen, their block might protect the capturing piece, or their queen might capture the checking piece. If you simply assume that everything stays in place while you insert a check, your analysis is incomplete. The intermediate move creates another turn for the opponent as well as another opportunity for you. Reconstruct the position after their strongest response.

A useful intermediate move can improve the recapture itself. Suppose your immediate pawn recapture would create a weak structure, but a forcing exchange first lets a bishop recapture instead. Or a check may drive a king away so that your queen can recover material with an additional threat. The gain may be positional rather than a net material profit. The important point is that changing the order produces a better final position than the automatic response.

Intermediate captures can remove a defender before the expected recapture. Your opponent takes a rook, assuming you will recover their attacking piece. Instead, you may capture the defender of their queen with check, making the queen vulnerable. Such combinations must be counted carefully because several valuable pieces may be under attack simultaneously. Write the material balance mentally at the end of each plausible branch. The dramatic interruption is useful only if its final outcome is better than the alternatives.

A quiet intermediate move is possible when it creates a threat more urgent than the existing problem. For example, a move may threaten unavoidable mate while your queen is attacked. The opponent must address the mating threat if it is real. This is more difficult to verify than an ordinary check because the opponent may have several ways to continue with threats of their own. Check whether the supposed mate is actually forced and whether the attacked queen can survive or be given up profitably.

Checks are natural candidates, but they are not automatically good intermediate moves. A check can drive the king to safety, help the opponent develop a piece by blocking, or move your queen away from an important defensive duty. If the check gains nothing and worsens the final recapture, it may be an unnecessary detour. Judge what the opponent's forced response changes. The value lies in the altered position, not in the fact that the move came with check.

A check that attacks another target is especially promising. A queen may check the king and simultaneously threaten a rook. A knight may give check while forking another valuable piece, meaning it attacks both targets at once. A discovered check may reveal a rook's attack as another piece moves with a threat. These patterns can make a delayed recapture profitable because the opponent has to deal with more than one urgent issue. Every capture and escape still needs examination.

Intermediate moves can also save an attacked piece. If the opponent attacks your queen, you may have a check with another piece that forces the enemy queen to move or removes the attacker. After the forcing reply, your queen may escape with tempo or capture something valuable. But if the opponent can answer the check while continuing to attack your queen, you may have achieved nothing. Compare the queen's situation before and after the interruption rather than assuming that any forcing move buys useful time.

An in between move may reveal that recapture is unnecessary. Perhaps the opponent's capturing piece is trapped and cannot escape, so you have time to make a stronger threat first. Perhaps taking the enemy queen is more valuable than recovering a bishop. Perhaps a mating sequence ends the game before the material deficit matters. The principle is to compare the urgency and value of available actions, not to treat recapturing as an obligation that must eventually be fulfilled.

At other times, delaying loses the recapture completely. The opponent may move the capturing piece with check during the inserted sequence, or exchange your intended recapturing piece. Then your intermediate idea may leave you down material without compensation. Ask whether the original target can escape and whether you still need to recover it. If the combination's profit depends on a later recapture, that recapture must remain legal and effective after the opponent's best reply.

Move order is the heart of the tactic. Compare recapturing first with inserting the forcing move first. What does the immediate recapture change? It may close a line needed for the check or remove a target that made a fork possible. What does the intermediate move change? It may force a king move, pull a defender away, or improve your piece's placement. Understanding these differences is more useful than merely remembering that an unexpected move exists in the position.

Sometimes the opponent can answer your intermediate move with one of their own. You give check before recapturing, and their legal response to check attacks your queen. You threaten mate, and they force a queen exchange with check. Tactical sequences are not one sided scripts. At every turn, the player to move may have a stronger forcing action than the one you expected. This is why good calculation repeatedly asks for the opponent's best resource rather than following a single natural line.

The concept applies in endgames as well. A king may use a forcing capture to gain a tempo before taking another pawn. A rook may give check to drive the enemy king away from a promotion square before recovering material. A pawn may promote with check before responding to an attack elsewhere. Small timing changes can decide whether a race is won or drawn. Count legal moves precisely, including whether the new promoted piece can be captured immediately.

Pawn races particularly reward attention to check. If both sides are close to promotion, one pawn may promote with check and force the opponent to respond before promoting their own pawn. That can change the outcome. But it is not a universal winning formula: the checked side may capture the promoted piece, block effectively, or still reach a favorable result. The intermediate move idea tells you to compare the forcing order, while exact calculation determines the race.

Defensive intermediate moves can rescue positions that look lost. Your queen may be attacked while a rook is hanging, meaning undefended and available for capture. A forcing check could enable a queen exchange, remove the threat to the rook, or create perpetual checking chances. Perpetual checking chances involve repeated checks that may prevent the opponent from making progress. Before resigning a material loss in your mind, inspect forcing resources. A position's apparent obligations may be changed by one accurate interruption.

This does not mean you should search endlessly for miracles after every threat. Use warning signs to focus attention: exposed kings, loose pieces, open lines, and defenders with several urgent jobs. If none of these exists and your threatened piece has a straightforward safe move, the ordinary response may be best. The habit is a brief inspection before automatic play, with deeper calculation when the position offers a concrete reason. Efficient tactical awareness combines curiosity with proportionate effort.

When planning your own capture, look for the opponent's possible intermediate reply before making it. Ask what lines your capture opens and what defensive task your piece abandons. If taking a bishop exposes your king to a checking fork, the apparent material gain may disappear. This preventive use of the idea is often more valuable than spotting an occasional spectacular move. It reduces the number of positions where you are surprised by a forcing response you could have anticipated.

A helpful verbal method is to state the expected move and then ask what is more urgent. I expect to recapture the knight, but do I have a check that wins something first? I expect the opponent to take my rook, but can they instead check my king and attack my queen? The wording keeps the search connected to the actual position. You are testing the move order, not abandoning calculation in favor of random aggression.

After finding a candidate, compare at least the main defensive choices. Can the checking piece be captured? Can a line check be blocked? Does the king have several safe moves? Can the opponent answer a threat with a stronger threat? Then evaluate the final position, including the original material issue. The strongest intermediate move is the one that improves the outcome against best defense, not the one that produces the prettiest line after the most natural response.

The first practice question begins after the opponent captures your bishop with check. You notice that your queen could attack the enemy queen elsewhere, but that move would not remove the check against your king. Can it be played as an in between move? Take a moment to distinguish a powerful threat from a legal response to check.

No. Every legal move must remove the check when your king is attacked. You must move the king safely, capture the checking piece, or block the checking line if that is possible. Your queen attack is irrelevant if it leaves your king in check. A move that both resolves the check and creates a stronger threat could be an intermediate resource, but the unrelated attack described in the question is not legal.

For the second question, you can recapture a pawn immediately or first give check. The opponent's best response to the check develops a bishop, protects the pawn you wanted to recover, and leaves your queen less active. The check wins no material and creates no new threat. Is inserting it useful simply because the opponent must respond? Think about the quality of the resulting position rather than the forcing appearance of the move.

No. The check may improve the opponent's coordination and make your original task harder. A forcing move is valuable only if the forced response benefits you or creates an adequate opportunity. Here, the described effects suggest that recapturing immediately or finding another candidate may be stronger. This is an important discipline: intermediate moves are not decorative checks placed before normal play. They should change the move order for a concrete reason.

The third question starts with an enemy knight that has captured your rook. You can recapture the knight, but you first see a queen check that would also attack an undefended enemy bishop. After the opponent's strongest legal reply, your queen can win that bishop, and the knight remains trapped and recoverable. What makes the intermediate check worth investigating? Give yourself a moment to connect the extra gain with the preservation of the original recapture.

The check may gain additional material without losing the opportunity to recover the knight. Its force makes the opponent address the king, while the double attack creates a second target. The knight's inability to escape is essential to the stated benefit. You still need to verify the legal replies and the final material count, including the rook already lost. The coherent idea is that delaying the natural recapture improves the result because the original recovery remains available afterward.

To practice, take tactical exercises that begin with a capture and deliberately list forcing alternatives before choosing the obvious recapture. Explain what each check or strong capture would change. Then discard the ones that help the defender, lose the original recovery, or fail to meet an existing check. This process teaches discrimination. You learn to recognize useful interruptions while also understanding why many possible intermediate moves should not be played.

Review your own games for moments when you replied instantly to a capture or queen attack. Pause the replay and inspect the position as though the expected response were only one candidate. Look for an exposed king, a loose piece, or a line opened by the last move. You may find missed opportunities or simply confirm that your response was correct. Both outcomes strengthen the habit of checking the move order before committing to it.

An in between move challenges the assumption that the obvious response must come next. It works by creating a more urgent demand and improving the position before the original issue is resolved. Check legality, compare forcing replies, preserve any recovery you still need, and count the final result. With this habit, you will become less vulnerable to automatic sequences and more alert to the move that changes the entire order of the conversation between the two armies.
